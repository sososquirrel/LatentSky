%% ===================== 1. INTRODUCTION =====================================
\section{Introduction}

How far ahead can precipitation be predicted? The honest answer depends on the
rain: a 500-km monsoon rain shield will still be found eight hours from now; a
30-km convective cell may not survive the next one. That predictability grows
with spatial scale is one of the oldest results of predictability theory
\cite{lorenz1969}, and its convective-scale limit has been studied extensively
in idealized and case settings \cite{hohenegger2007, zhang2003}. Yet the way
precipitation forecasts are routinely evaluated --- pixel-wise scores averaged
over a domain --- collapses this scale dependence into a single number and, worse,
biases the comparison itself: a model trained and scored on mean squared error
returns the conditional mean of an increasingly wide predictive distribution,
a smooth field whose systems are too large and too weak. Pixel scores reward
exactly this blur (the double-penalty problem \cite{ravuri2021}), so the
apparent predictability limit mixes two things of very different nature: what
the atmosphere forbids, and what the estimator destroys.

The systems in question matter well beyond forecast verification. Deep
convection organizes into mesoscale convective systems spanning
${\sim}100$~km --- key contributors to tropical extreme weather, from intense
gust fronts to heavy rainfall and flash flooding, and a major term in the
global water cycle. Their typical scale falls below the grid of global climate
models, which represent deep convection through parameterizations, so their
initiation, development, and response to climate change remain highly
uncertain. Global storm-resolving simulations now resolve these systems
explicitly: the DYAMOND protocol \cite{stevens2019} collects kilometer-scale
integrations from more than nine models, producing petabytes of data at
${\sim}4$~km resolution. But realism alone does not guarantee understanding;
datasets of this kind call for analytical frameworks posed at the scale of the
systems themselves, and the scale-decomposed horizon of this paper is one such
framework --- it asks the predictability question system by system, size by
size.

Two distinct barriers stand between a forecast and the rain. The first is
\emph{resolution}: the processes that decide whether convection fires ---
microphysics, boundary-layer thermals, cold pools --- live below the grid of
climate and reanalysis products and are parameterized or absent, so small
systems cannot even be represented, let alone predicted. The second is
\emph{chaos}: even when convection is explicitly resolved, small convective
patterns are inherently unstable --- they decorrelate within the hour, and no
increase in resolution removes this limit. This paper is built to isolate the
second barrier and measure it. Training and evaluating on kilometer-scale
data takes the resolution barrier out of the problem as far as currently
possible; what remains, quantified by the horizon $T(L)$, is the intrinsic
unpredictability of the systems themselves. The question then becomes what a
machine-learning model can still do against chaos --- and the answer this
paper defends is: be probabilistic. A deterministic network, asked for the
one true field, can only return the average of all plausible outcomes --- the
blur. A generative model can instead return sharp \emph{samples} of those
outcomes: the chaos does not disappear, but it moves from an error stamped
into the field to a spread across draws, where it can be measured, priced,
and used.

A second confusion hides in how precipitation is produced by modern forecast
systems. Most AI weather models advance the atmospheric \emph{state}
autoregressively and obtain precipitation either by diagnosing it from the
predicted state with a separate network \cite{bi2023} or by predicting it
jointly with the other targets \cite{price2025}, typically at 3--6~h steps.
Their precipitation error therefore compounds two sources: error in the state,
and the irreducible uncertainty of mapping state to rain. This paper isolates
the second. Every model we train receives the \emph{simultaneous},
kilometer-scale-resolved large-scale state as input --- the question it
answers is: even if the state variables were known perfectly, how far into
the future is precipitation itself predictable? The horizon we measure is
conditional on perfect state knowledge and is therefore an upper bound: no
autoregressive system that derives rain from these state variables can exceed
it, because it reflects the stochasticity of the state-to-rain map, not
errors in state prediction.

This paper separates the two, at kilometer scale, with an instrument simple
enough to state in one identity. Every precipitating system --- a connected
rainy object --- carries a total precipitation $P$ that factors exactly into an
area and an area-weighted intensity, $P = \rho A$, so that for a matched
truth--forecast pair the logarithmic error splits with no cross term:
$\mathrm{d}\ln P = \mathrm{d}\ln A + \mathrm{d}\ln\rho$. Object-based
verification has a long tradition \cite{davis2006, wernli2008}; what we add is
to use it to \emph{define a horizon}. Binning systems by their size
$L = \sqrt{A}$ and fitting the decay of the probability of detection with lead
time yields an e-folding time $T(L)$ per scale band --- the detection horizon
--- measured identically for every forecast, including the persistence null,
which each of our models receives as an input so that the null is nested by
construction. The decomposition maps onto the two physically distinct
challenges of precipitation prediction: the \emph{trigger} --- whether and
where convection initiates, governed by boundary-layer thermals, cold-pool
collisions, and mesoscale convergence that are effectively stochastic at the
resolved scale --- and the \emph{amplitude} --- how intense it is, largely
controlled by the thermodynamic environment and inheriting its longer memory.
Detection (POD, and hence the horizon $T(L)$) measures the trigger;
the matched-pair errors $\mathrm{d}\ln A$ and $\mathrm{d}\ln\rho$ measure the
amplitude and its spatial organization, conditional on the trigger being
found. At zero lead the inputs read the trigger directly --- outgoing longwave
radiation is a near-direct observation of where deep convection is active ---
so the decay of detection with lead is precisely the timescale over which
trigger information is lost.

Three questions organize the paper. First, \emph{what sets the limit}: measured
on a storm-resolving DYAMOND integration \cite{stevens2019}, the horizon follows
a power law of system size, $T(L) \propto L^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha = 1.79$ for
the deterministic model and $1.41$ for persistence. Second, \emph{how much of
the limit is the estimator}: the decomposition shows the deterministic model's
compensating pathology --- systems too large ($\mathrm{d}\ln A > 0$) and too
weak ($\mathrm{d}\ln\rho < 0$), invisible to aggregate scores --- and a
conditional rectified flow \cite{lipman2023, liu2023} trained to transport the
forecast to the truth along its own measured error removes it, becoming the
first forecast above the persistence null at every scale, at an explicitly
priced false-alarm cost. Third, \emph{whether the limits transfer}: applied
unchanged to four operational AI weather models from WeatherBench~2
\cite{rasp2024} --- GraphCast \cite{lam2023}, FuXi \cite{chen2023}, GenCast
\cite{price2025}, and FGN --- the same scale story holds, per-draw sampling of
the generative member restores intensity, and below ${\sim}60$~km no model
detects anything at $0.25^{\circ}$, the argument that kilometer-scale training
data is necessary.

A word on ambition. The models trained here are deliberately modest --- a
$125$~M-parameter U-Net and its flow correction, trained on 40 days of data on
a single GPU --- and we make no claim to compete with operational systems
trained on decades of reanalysis at vastly larger compute. They are the
instrument's controlled experiment, not its product: small enough that every
design choice is inspectable, matched to each other so that the only
difference between the deterministic and generative forecast is the training
objective. The contribution is the measurement --- a scaling law
$T(L) \propto L^{\alpha}$ and its decomposition into what the atmosphere
forbids and what the estimator destroys --- and the framework that produces
it, which applies unchanged to any forecast, as Section~\ref{sec:res-wb2}
demonstrates on the operational models themselves.

Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the data, the two models, and the
evaluation; Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the three results in that order;
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what the horizon does and does not
measure.

The dataset is the DYAMOND MPAS-Atmosphere global cloud-resolving simulation
\cite{stevens2019}, performed at ${\sim}3.8$~km horizontal resolution with
15-minute output over a 40-day winter period. MPAS employs a fully
compressible, nonhydrostatic dynamical core on an unstructured mesh, enabling
explicit simulation of convective processes; cloud microphysics are
represented with a double-moment scheme for cloud and rain hydrometeors.
Global cloud-resolving models explicitly resolve deep convection and cloud
systems, providing dynamically consistent four-dimensional fields essential
for process understanding and data-driven modeling \cite{satoh2019}, though
they remain model-dependent: MPAS reproduces mean precipitation and its
diurnal cycle within the multi-model range \cite{stevens2019} but tends to
overestimate deep convective clouds and heavy rainfall compared with the IMERG
product \cite{feng2025}. For this study the fields are regridded to a regular
$0.25^{\circ}$ grid (${\approx}28$~km) over the tropical belt
$40^{\circ}$S--$40^{\circ}$N: 40 days of $96$ frames on a $320 \times 1440$
grid. The kilometer-scale origin of the data matters more than the evaluation
grid: the target field contains the sharp, small, short-lived systems that
parameterized or coarse reanalysis products smooth away --- precisely the
systems whose predictability we want to measure.

Both models receive the identical ten input channels: precipitable water, 2-m
temperature, top-of-atmosphere outgoing longwave radiation, moist static energy
and horizontal winds at 850 and 500~hPa, and the current rain field itself
(log-transformed). The last channel is a deliberate design choice, not leakage:
every model is handed the persistence forecast as an input, so the persistence
null is nested --- any skill above it is information the model added. The
target is the raw rain field (mm per 15~min) at one of six trained leads,
$0.25$, $0.5$, $1$, $2$, $3$, and $6$~h; one model is trained per lead. Training
operates on $128 \times 128$ non-overlapping patches ($22$ blocks per frame).
The train/validation/test split assigns each of the $880$ (block, day) units to
one split for all $96$ frames of its day ($70/15/15$; $132$ test blocks), which
closes both spatial and temporal-autocorrelation leakage; construction details
and the residual-risk analysis are in Supporting Information Text~S1.

\subsection{Deterministic model: a two-head U-Net}\label{sec:unet}

The deterministic forecast is a U-Net (125~M parameters; five encoder--decoder
stages, $64$ to $1024$ channels, $2048$-channel bottleneck) with two parallel
output heads: a non-negative rain-intensity head and a wet/dry classification
head. The loss combines a masked intensity MSE on wet pixels, a
binary cross-entropy on the wet/dry head over the full field, and a
differentiable fractions-skill-score term evaluated at six kernel sizes chosen
to coincide with the $L$ bins of the evaluation ($28$--$900$~km), so the
training loss and the verification share one scale vocabulary. A FiLM layer
conditions the bottleneck on the patch's OLR statistics, a proxy for the
convective regime. Architecture, loss weights, and training schedule are given
in Text~S2.

Trained with MSE on intensity, this model returns the conditional mean of the
predictive distribution. As lead grows the distribution widens and the mean
blurs: this is not a defect of the architecture but a property of the loss, and
it is the specific pathology the evaluation of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}
isolates and the model of Section~\ref{sec:flow} corrects.

\subsection{Generative correction: a conditional rectified flow}\label{sec:flow}

The rectified flow learns the transport from the deterministic forecast to the
truth, and returns sharp samples of the conditional distribution instead of its
mean. In log-rain space, the source is the forecast perturbed by its own error,
$x_0 = z_{\mathrm{fc}} + \sigma \varepsilon$ with
$\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, where $\sigma$ is not a tuning knob but
the measured wet-masked forecast error (rms) at that lead ($0.10$ at $+15$~min
to $0.20$ at $+6$~h). Training draws $t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$, forms the
straight-line interpolant $x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1$ toward the truth $x_1$,
and regresses a velocity network $\hat{v}(x_t, t, c)$ --- the same U-Net trunk,
conditioned on the ten input channels $c$ and on $t$ --- onto the constant
displacement $v = x_1 - x_0$. Sampling integrates
$\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t = \hat{v}$ from a fresh draw of $x_0$ to $t=1$ with
$32$ steps of Heun's method --- the second-order Runge--Kutta
predictor--corrector: a trial Euler step with the velocity at the current
state, then an update with the average of the velocities at the current and
trial states ($\Delta t = 1/32$, two network evaluations per step). The noise scale is the entire source of stochasticity:
$\sigma \to 0$ collapses the flow to a deterministic regression, and each new
$\varepsilon$ yields a different sharp realization consistent with the same
smooth forecast. Figure~\ref{fig:flowmethod} shows the method on one example:
the smooth two-head forecast, two sharp flow draws consistent with it, and the
truth they are distributed around.

One scoring rule governs every generative result in this paper: metrics are
computed per draw and then averaged over draws, never on the draw-averaged
field. The mean field is again a conditional-mean estimate; scoring it would
turn the generative model back into the regression and erase exactly the
difference we measure. (A denoising diffusion model warm-started from the same
forecast was evaluated as a sampling-protocol sensitivity; it reaches a
small-scale power ceiling of $0.80$--$0.85$ attributable to its train/test
mismatch and is reported in Text~S3.)

\subsection{Evaluation: the $\rho A$ decomposition on matched pairs}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Systems are detected independently in the truth and forecast fields as
8-connected components above $0.1$~mm, with all areas latitude-weighted. Each
system carries $A$ (area, km$^2$), $P$ (total precipitation, mm\,km$^2$),
$\rho = P/A$ (area-weighted intensity, which makes $P = \rho A$ exact), and its
scale $L = \sqrt{A}$.

Matching is by spatial overlap within each frame: intersection-over-union is
computed between every truth--forecast pair of systems, and pairs are accepted
greedily in descending IoU order, one-to-one, down to $\mathrm{IoU} \ge 0.1$.
Every system then has a role: \emph{matched} (both sides exist), \emph{missed}
(truth system with no counterpart), or \emph{false alarm} (forecast system with
no counterpart). Misses and false alarms give the probability of detection
(POD) and false-alarm ratio (FAR) per log-spaced $L$ bin; matched pairs give
the log-additive error decomposition
$\mathrm{d}\ln P = \mathrm{d}\ln A + \mathrm{d}\ln\rho$, in which negative
means underestimated and the log form weighs a $+50\%$ error identically on a
$30$-km cell and a $400$-km mesoscale system. The horizon is then defined per
scale band as the e-folding time of detection,
$\mathrm{POD}(t; L) = \mathrm{POD}_0(L)\, e^{-t/\tau(L)}$, fitted across all
six leads. Persistence is evaluated under the identical detection, matching,
and fitting; because long-lived storms still overlap their past selves at loose
IoU, persistence is a genuinely strong null for detection, and every
sensitivity of the matching (IoU threshold, rain threshold, many-to-one
matching) is reported in Text~S4.

Figure~\ref{fig:pairs}A shows the instrument at work on one frame; the
statistics that follow pool ${\sim}10^6$ such systems. Fitted over the full
held-out record ($132$ test blocks $\times$ $40$ days;
Figure~\ref{fig:horizon}), the detection horizon rises from
$\tau \approx 0.5$~h for the smallest resolved systems ($L \approx 30$~km) to
beyond a day for $L > 400$~km, and follows a power law
$T(L) \propto L^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha = 1.79$ for the two-head model and
$\alpha = 1.41$ for persistence. The two curves cross near $L \approx 100$~km:
above it the model separates cleanly from the null, below it persistence wins
--- small, short-lived convection is where the deterministic forecast has
nothing to add over the observation that storms briefly persist.

The decomposition says why aggregate scores miss this
(Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}A). At $+3$~h the matched
pairs of the two-head model sit in a quadrant of compensating errors: band
barycenters $(\mathrm{d}\ln A, \mathrm{d}\ln\rho) = (+0.53, -1.05)$ for small
systems, $(+0.14, -0.75)$ at mid scales, $(+0.20, -0.39)$ for large ---
systems systematically too large and too weak, with the two errors partially
canceling in $\mathrm{d}\ln P$. This is the signature of the conditional mean,
and it grows toward small scales, exactly where the horizon is shortest.

\subsection{What the flow restores}\label{sec:res-flow}

The rectified flow removes the pathology it was trained on. In the
decomposition plane (Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}B, axes identical to panel~A) the
$+3$~h band barycenters collapse toward the origin: small systems from
$(+0.53, -1.05)$ to $(+0.21, -0.24)$, mid from $(+0.14, -0.75)$ to
$(-0.15, -0.15)$, large from $(+0.20, -0.39)$ to $(-0.14, -0.04)$. Size and
intensity are restored at every scale, per draw --- these are single sharp
realizations, not an ensemble mean.

Detection improves with representation. On the example frame
(Figure~\ref{fig:pairs}B) small systems appear where the two-head proposed
none; over the full record (Figure~\ref{fig:horizon}A) the flow's POD exceeds
the two-head's in every scale band and at every lead ($0.79$ vs $0.72$ at
$L \approx 190$~km, $+3$~h), and for the smallest band the POD nearly triples
($0.078$ vs $0.028$). The horizon steepens to $\alpha = 1.63$
(Figure~\ref{fig:horizon}C) and, most importantly, sits above
\emph{both} the deterministic model and persistence at every scale
($\tau = 0.83$~h vs $0.53$/$0.55$~h at $L \approx 30$~km): the
$100$-km crossover disappears, making the flow the first forecast in this study
to beat the nested null everywhere. The instrument prices the cost in the same
breath: in the smallest band the tripled detection comes with FAR $0.92$ vs
$0.64$ --- the flow finds small storms by proposing many, and we state this as
the trade it is rather than hiding it in an aggregate score.

The evaluation transfers unchanged to WeatherBench~2
(Figures~\ref{fig:wb2maps}--\ref{fig:wb2clouds}): GraphCast, FuXi, GenCast,
and FGN against ERA5 over the same tropical belt, at rate-equivalent thresholds
($2.4$~mm per $6$~h $=$ the km-scale $0.1$~mm per $15$~min). This part is an
outlook, not a ranking against LatentSky --- different data, resolution, and
leads. Three observations carry it.

First, the identical scale story: POD rises monotonically with $L$ for every
model, and below $L \approx 60$~km every model --- deterministic or generative
--- sits at $\mathrm{POD} \le 0.13$ at $+6$ and $+12$~h, already a medium lead
for convection. Second, sampling helps where the models are competent:
at $+12$~h a single GenCast member gives the highest POD of any forecast from
${\approx}240$~km up ($0.78$--$0.82$ vs GraphCast's $0.68$--$0.73$), at a FAR
cost ($0.32$ vs $0.17$), while the GenCast ensemble mean falls back into the
deterministic pack --- the per-draw scoring rule matters as much for
operational models as for ours. Third, over a full month of matched systems
(${\sim}8{,}000$ pairs per model), the deterministic models carry a systematic
too-weak bias worst at small scales ($\mathrm{d}\ln\rho$ barycenters $-0.24$,
$-0.17$, $-0.06$ from small to large bands), and the diffusion member erases it
at every scale ($-0.06$, $-0.03$, $+0.01$). The error clouds are wide in
$\mathrm{d}\ln A$ and narrow in $\mathrm{d}\ln\rho$ --- the reverse of the
km-scale clouds: at $0.25^{\circ}$ the rain rate is recoverable but the system
boundaries are not.

The small-system floor itself, however, is not an indictment of resolution
alone --- our own scaling law prices the lead. With
$\tau(30~\mathrm{km}) \approx 0.8$~h even for the flow at kilometer scale, a
$+6$~h forecast asks for detection seven e-folding times past the horizon: no
model, at any resolution, should find a $30$-km system at $+6$ or $+12$~h.
The two arguments therefore compose rather than compete. Kilometer-scale data
is necessary for small systems to be \emph{representable} at all, and
sub-hour forecast cadence is necessary to catch them \emph{within their
horizon}; operational systems stepping at $6$~h fail both conditions at once,
and fixing the resolution without the cadence would move nothing below
${\sim}60$~km.
